Automata, A to Z

A complete, intuition-first course on automata theory, formal languages, computability and complexity. Every idea comes with the why before the what, a precise definition, worked examples, machines you can run in your browser, and exercises with solutions.

What you will be able to do

  • Design finite automata, regular expressions, grammars, pushdown automata and Turing machines for a given language — and know which model is the right one.
  • Convert between models: NFA → DFA, regex ↔ automaton, CFG ↔ PDA, any grammar → Chomsky Normal Form, and minimize a DFA.
  • Prove limits: show a language is not regular (pumping, Myhill–Nerode, closure) or not context-free, and show a problem is undecidable (diagonalization, reductions, Rice's theorem).
  • Reason about efficiency: P, NP, NP-completeness, polynomial-time reductions, and the space classes.
  • Recognise the theory in practice: lexers, regex engines, parsers, protocol state machines, static analysis and verification.
The one-sentence story of this whole subject

Give a machine more memory and it can recognise more patterns — finite memory gives regular languages, a stack gives context-free languages, an unbounded tape gives everything computable — and yet even the most powerful machine has problems it can never solve, and many it can solve but not quickly.

The course map

Suggested paths

  • Taking a university course? Read in order. Chapters 3–10 are typically the first half of the term, 11–15 the second.
  • Preparing for an exam in a hurry? Read every Intuition and Takeaways box, then do the exercises in Chapter 17 and return to whatever you get wrong.
  • A working programmer who wants the CS foundations? Chapters 1, 3, 4, 5, 8, 11, 12, 15 and 16 give the highest return per hour.

Prerequisites

Very few. You should be comfortable with basic programming and with reading simple mathematical notation. Everything else — sets, functions, proof by induction, proof by contradiction, countability — is taught from scratch in Chapter 2.
